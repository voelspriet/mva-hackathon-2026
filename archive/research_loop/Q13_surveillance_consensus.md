# Q13. What does the 2025 consensus say, and is the report using the right document?

**Asked because** the report's four surveillance additions and its imaging rule rest on one paper, and a Google Scholar sweep surfaced a 2025 AACR consensus series the project had never found. If the report is anchored to the wrong document, the surveillance section is the most exposed part of it.

## Answer

**The report is already anchored to the right document.** That is worth stating plainly, because the worry that prompted this question was that the project lacked clinical grounding.

`Nakano 2024, PMID 39264246` is not a stray citation. It is **"Update on Recommendations for Cancer Screening and Surveillance in Children with Genomic Instability Disorders", Clin Cancer Res 2024;30(22):5009-5020**, the genomic-instability paper of the AACR Childhood Cancer Predisposition series, authored by Nakano, Kuiper, Nichols, Porter, Kratz, Plon, Walsh and others across SickKids, Princess Máxima, St Jude, Emory and Baylor. Mosaic variegated aneuploidy is a genomic instability disorder. This is the per-syndrome consensus for exactly this child, and the report has been citing it throughout.

**What was missing is the series summary**, published a year later: **Brodeur GM, Diller LR, Nichols KE, Plon SE, Porter CC, Malkin D. "Pediatric Cancer Predisposition and Surveillance Update: Summary Perspective and Future Directions." Clin Cancer Res 2025;31(13):2581-2588, PMC12213195, open access.** It adds two things the report needs and one it should quote.

### 1. A threshold, which the report's surveillance additions never had

Under "Cancer Surveillance Considerations":

> "In general, a 5% or greater chance of developing a childhood cancer in a particular age window was considered a reasonable threshold to recommend screening, but lower thresholds (≥1%) were considered in some conditions."

**This is a decision rule the report can use and does not have.** MVA1's published cancer proportion is 38.7 percent (Pavone 2022, 12 of 31), which is roughly eight times the threshold at which this consensus considers screening reasonable, and thirty-eight times the lower threshold used in some conditions. Even the most conservative reading of the risk literature clears it by a wide margin. The report's surveillance additions were argued from mechanism and from the fact that they cost nothing. They can now be argued from the same threshold the field uses.

### 2. A more precise statement of what surveillance is claimed to achieve

Under "Evidence that Surveillance Improves Outcome":

> "Together, these data on cancer surveillance in pediatric patients with a CPS suggest that screening enables detection of smaller tumors, allowing for less intensive therapy, less organ toxicity, and better outcomes."

The report's lay summary says surveillance "has not been shown to change how many children die". That remains true, and no mortality claim is made in the consensus either. But it is incomplete as written, because it implies the benefit is unproven in general when the claimed benefit is **stage shift and treatment de-escalation**: smaller tumours, less intensive therapy, less organ toxicity. For a child who has already had rhabdomyosarcoma and whose syndrome may make cytotoxic therapy worse to tolerate, less intensive therapy is not a lesser benefit than survival. It may be the more relevant one.

### 3. The epistemic status of MVA surveillance, in the consensus authors' own words

Under "Consistent Approach to Surveillance":

> "For some syndromes, no surveillance guidelines existed, so the working groups devised protocols based on available information and technology... These protocols, in particular, will be re-evaluated and optimized once there is sufficient experience to determine their effectiveness."

That is a fair description of where MVA surveillance stands, written by the people who wrote it, and it is a better citation for the report's own caution than anything the report currently uses. It also licenses the report's approach: the consensus itself devises protocols from available information for syndromes with no evidence base, which is what this report did.

## Corrections applied

The lay summary now says what surveillance is claimed to achieve rather than only what it has not been shown to do. The gate's surveillance additions gain the 5 percent threshold as an anchor, with the note that MVA1 clears it by roughly eightfold. Section 12 gains the consensus authors' own characterisation of protocols devised without an evidence base.

## Access levels reached

Brodeur 2025 full text via PMC, open access. Nakano 2024 verified at abstract and author-list level via NCBI efetch; it was already in the report's source set. The companion imaging paper in the same series (AJR special series on screening) and the Wilms tumour predisposition update (Clin Cancer Res 31:18) were not reached: both return HTTP 403 to automated fetch and neither is in PMC. They are added to the paywalled list.
