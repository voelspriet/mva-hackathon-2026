# Round 2: paediatric clinical pharmacology

## (a) Objections

**Geneticist.** Top candidate, potassium citrate with a conditional thiazide: you rank it first because its target is measured in this child, then defer the urine work-up that decides the drug. Alkali is the treatment for distal renal tubular acidosis and the wrong first move for a proximal tubulopathy; citrate at 0.1 to 0.2 g/kg/day also delivers a potassium load, and your own limit of urine pH 7.5 (02 §3) cannot be titrated before the lesion is characterised. The work-up is the entry, not the prescription.
Second claim: "Sirolimus: same mechanism as everolimus without the paediatric formulation" is wrong. 02 §5 records routine off-label paediatric sirolimus in complicated vascular anomalies from infancy (Adams 2016, PMID 26783326). What everolimus adds is an approved paediatric indication with trough targets, not the existence of a formulation.

**Cell biologist.** Top candidate, dasatinib at rank 1 because "the target, not the drug, has model-level causal support": the dose does not follow from that. 40 to 100 mg daily by weight band (02 §5) is a continuous antileukaemic schedule, while the senolytic design you invoke is Xu 2018's intermittent mouse regimen (01 §4.2), and no human senolytic dose exists at any age in any condition (02 §6). Rank 1 names a target without a regimen.
Second claim: pimitespib at rank 5 is outside the brief. It is approved in Japan only, for advanced GIST, with no paediatric data (01 §5), and by your own note HSP90 governs turnover of destabilised BubR1 missense protein (01 §2.2), so it would lower residual BubR1 if p.Asn1002Lys is in that class.

**Oncologist.** Top candidate, thiazide plus citrate ranked first partly to preserve renal reserve for ifosfamide, cyclophosphamide and platinum: a thiazide works against that goal, because volume contraction is part of its calcium effect (02 §3), and hyperhydration-dependent alkylator therapy is the setting where hypokalaemia and volume depletion are least wanted. The sequencing has to be stated: hold the thiazide through any cytotoxic block, continue the citrate.
Second claim: dexrazoxane at rank 2 rests on cardiac death in BubR1^H/H mice and the J-point defect (01 §4.1). Neither is anthracycline injury. Dexrazoxane acts on topoisomerase IIβ and iron-mediated damage, so the organ overlaps and the mechanism does not; the tightness you claim for the link is not there.

**Family representative.** Top candidate, ranked first for "zero extra visits": thiazide plus citrate needs serum potassium after initiation and after each increment, plus urine pH titration (02 §3), so it adds draws and home testing between the three-monthly scans. The entry still ranks first for me, on a corrected burden statement rather than that one.
Second claim: you credit metformin with "decades of paediatric safety" at rank 2. Approval begins at age 10 (02 §5); below that the only paediatric dosing evidence is a phase I in relapsed tumours at 666 to 2,000 mg/m²/day, and the GI intolerance you name as the harm is the usual reason that dose is not reached.

## (b) What changes my ranking

Three arguments move me. No clone exceeds about 10% in blood (cell biologist, point 5), so an aneuploidy-selective drug may have little target in the compartment I proposed to sample. The oncologist and the family both put hydroxychloroquine's dose-limiting toxicity on an organ MVA already affects (cataract, corneal opacity, glaucoma). And the geneticist's measurement gate precedes every mechanism-directed entry I listed.

1. Potassium citrate, thiazide added only after urine chemistry. Up from 2; I adopt the geneticist's condition on the thiazide.
2. Measurement gate, not a drug: BubR1 immunoblot, micronucleus and PCS rate, phasing. Adopted from the geneticist's entry 2.
3. Cyclophosphamide with mesna in place of ifosfamide at relapse. New, from the oncologist; the largest available protection of the GFR that every other entry depends on.
4. Hydroxychloroquine, conditional on a repeatable baseline ophthalmology assessment. Down from 1, on the ocular overlap and the missing blood target.
5. Metformin, unchanged in substance, with a cystatin C threshold set before the first dose.
6. Dexrazoxane if an anthracycline is given. New, on organ overlap only, with the mechanism claim removed.
Dasatinib moves to excluded: growth warning against short stature and failure to thrive, and no senolytic dose at any age.

## (c) Factual error

Cell biologist, framing section: "Both patient alleles act by lowering protein amount, not by making a poison (Suijkerbuijk 2010, PMID 20516114)". That is shown for truncating alleles and for the missense alleles that were tested, not for this one. 01 §7 lists "that p.(Asn1002Lys) belongs to the destabilising class" as inferred, and Q921H, also inside the kinase domain, was indistinguishable from wild type in the same paper (01 §1.3). No functional data on N1002K exist, so the premise that caps his candidate list is an assumption.

## (d) The disagreement not to paper over

Dasatinib is rank 1 in one file and last or excluded in three, including mine. The split is not about evidence tier: every file cites the same INK-ATTAC experiment and records that no senolytic has been given to a BubR1 mouse (01 §4.2). It is about direction of effect on tumour risk, and the cell biologist states the counter-argument himself: senescence and p53-limited proliferation may be restraining karyotypically complex cells in a child whose reported cancer risk runs from 38.7% to about 75% (02 §1). One experiment settles it, a senolytic in BubR1^H/H or ^H/L1002P mice with tumour incidence as a co-primary endpoint. The report should carry both positions with that experiment named, not a compromise mid-rank that neither side holds.
