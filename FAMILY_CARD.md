# One page for any doctor who prescribes

**For a child with mosaic variegated aneuploidy caused by changes in both copies of the *BUB1B* gene, who also has calcium deposits in the kidneys and has been treated for a soft-tissue cancer.**

This page exists so a parent does not have to explain a rare syndrome in a ten-minute appointment. It is written to be handed over. **Everything on it is graded by how strong the evidence actually is.**

---

## Read this first

> ## This page is NEVER a reason to change cancer treatment.
>
> It does not apply to chemotherapy or radiotherapy given to treat, control, or prevent the return of a cancer. **Do not reduce, delay, omit or swap any medicine in a cancer treatment plan because of anything written here.** Those decisions belong to the oncology team.
>
> It also does not apply to any medicine given urgently where no equivalent exists.

---

## Part 1. Well established, and the reason is the kidneys, not the syndrome

This child has calcium deposits in the kidneys. **Any** child with that finding warrants care with medicines that promote calcium loss or stones. This is ordinary paediatric practice and does not depend on the rare diagnosis at all.

| Medicine | Why | An alternative to discuss |
|---|---|---|
| Topiramate | Documented to cause kidney stones and calcium deposits in children, which resolved when it was stopped | Levetiracetam |
| Acetazolamide | Same mechanism as topiramate. Reasoned from how the drug works, not from studies in children | Ask the team |
| A water tablet (loop diuretic), when not urgent | A well-known cause of kidney calcium deposits in babies | A thiazide diuretic |
| High-dose intermittent vitamin D | Linked to later kidney calcium deposits in a small series of ten children. The authors called it a suggestion, not proof | Daily vitamin D, with the blood level measured |

**Also well established:** growth hormone and mecasermin should only ever be started through the endocrine team. There is no evidence they help in this syndrome, and one of them carries a formal warning in a child who has had cancer.

## One separate finding, about blood clotting

The genome carries one copy of the commonest inherited clotting variant in people of European ancestry, called factor V Leiden. Both halves of what follows are well established.

- It is **common**. Roughly one person in 25 to 50 of European ancestry carries one copy, and most never have a clot.
- Carrying one copy is **not a reason to start any blood-thinning medicine**, and nothing about this child's care should change because of it.

It is written down for one reason only: if a clot ever happens, or if a long-term drip line into a vein is being planned, the team may want to know this is in the background. It is a fact for the file, not an action.

## Part 2. An open question, not an instruction

A few ordinary medicines interfere with the same step in cell division that this child's gene affects: **colchicine, griseofulvin, albendazole and mebendazole**.

**It is tempting to conclude that this child should avoid them. That conclusion is not supported, and it would be wrong to act on it as though it were.** Stated plainly:

- **Nobody has ever tested whether cells from a person with this syndrome are more sensitive to these medicines.** It has not been done.
- The two pieces of laboratory evidence that exist **point in opposite directions**. One suggests more chromosome errors. The other suggests these cells are harder to kill, not easier.
- What is known is that one of these drugs, albendazole, causes measurable chromosome damage in ordinary children at ordinary doses. That is true of **any** child, and says nothing about this one being more vulnerable.

**So this is a question worth raising with the specialist team, not a list to act on.** If this child needs treatment for worms, a fungal infection, or gout, **treat it**. Leaving a treatable infection untreated on the strength of an untested idea would be the greater harm.

## Part 3. Three questions worth asking the specialist team

**1. When was the calcium in the kidneys first seen on a scan? Was it on any scan before birth?**
The clinical summary says it was present at birth. The two usual explanations both happen after birth. Which one is correct changes which kidney medicine is right, and they point in opposite directions. One date from the records settles it.

**2. The *UGT1A1* check needs a different test from the one already done. Which test, and is it worth doing before irinotecan is ever needed?**
This is a safety check for one specific chemotherapy drug. An earlier version of this page said the answer could simply be looked up in sequencing already paid for. **That was wrong, and checking it is how we found out.** The main risk version of this gene is a short piece of repeated text in a switch region, and the sequencing method used here cannot read repeats of that kind. There is no result at that position, and a stretch of about 3,800 letters around it is blank. A separate small test reads it directly. A second, less common version of the same gene was readable, and it is absent.

**3. Given that the change is in this particular gene, how close should cancer surveillance be?**
Not every gene causing this syndrome carries the same cancer risk. Published work groups this gene with the higher-risk ones and recommends close surveillance for that group. Two additions cost nothing: a blood count added to a blood test already happening, and a preference for ultrasound or non-X-ray scans wherever either would answer the question.

## What nobody knows

There is no medicine that treats this syndrome. **Nothing on this page is expected to reduce this child's risk of cancer, and no test here can show whether it has.** Part 1 is ordinary good practice for a child with kidney calcium deposits. Part 2 is an open research question. Part 3 is three things to ask.

The reasoning behind every line, with sources and uncertainties, is in `REPORT_track2.md` in the accompanying repository.
