# Round 1, patient-family representative 2

Position: parent of a child with a different ultra-rare cancer-predisposition syndrome; now works on trial design, consent and off-label reimbursement for a European rare-disease umbrella body. EURORDIS-trained, with n-of-1 governance experience. Public material only, no contact with this family or the MVA Society, no web checks used.

Ranking rule for this seat: candidates are ordered by the outcomes families name in dossier 03 (symptom management first, cancer-risk reduction second, cure third, M3; three-monthly surveillance as today's main tool, M4 and M6; "constant care and attention", S2) and in the creative files (feeding, H5; weight velocity, MUAC and grip strength as family-held numbers, J13 and J14; hospital days, C9). A candidate that cannot move growth, feeding, renal burden, surveillance load or hospital days inside a year ranks below one that can, whatever its mechanism.

## My acceptable-risk line

I accept an existing paediatric label or a published paediatric dose, harms that are monitorable and reversible, and a plan that leaves cancer treatment options intact. I refuse a permanent growth deficit, a nephrotoxic agent in a child with nephrocalcinosis, chronic immunosuppression, and anything that raises missegregation, when the return offered is a biomarker. Dossier F section 4 is the reason: at a residual cancer-risk share of 20 to 30 percent, a 5 percent added harm needs a 22 to 64 percent relative cancer reduction to break even, and no approved drug has a measured effect on cancer incidence in MVA (dossier 02 section 6, zero registered trials). Cancer-risk arguments therefore cannot buy harm here. Only pre-specified present-day symptom endpoints can.

## 1. Ranked candidates (max 6)

**1. Hydrochlorothiazide plus potassium citrate.** Step: urinary calcium and crystal inhibition, not BubR1. Tier: human, in other diseases (thiazides arrest nephrocalcinosis progression in X-linked hypophosphataemia, PMID 11433085; dossier 02 section 3). Paediatric: licensed and weight-dosed, 0.5 to 1 mg/kg/day HCTZ and 0.3 to 0.6 mmol/kg/day citrate, both giveable at 15 kg (file I). Phenotype: treats the renal feature directly, the two agents offset on potassium, and all vitamin D and calcium supplements stop. Harm: hypokalaemia and volume depletion, poorly tolerated with failure to thrive; dose on cystatin C, since creatinine overreads at low muscle mass. Readout: urine calcium-to-creatinine at 0 and 6 to 12 weeks, potassium and bicarbonate at 1 and 4 weeks, ultrasound 6-monthly. Rank: the only item here that reaches the child this year with a paediatric dose.

**2. A written approved-drug avoidance rule.** Step: iatrogenic missegregation and hypercalciuria. Tier: human genotoxicity plus inference (colchicine as the OECD TG 487 aneugenic control, PMID 20362070; griseofulvin, PMID 25308540; topiramate, PMC5976810; loop diuretics as the strongest preterm nephrocalcinosis risk factor, file B2). Alternatives named: terbinafine, levetiracetam, daily rather than stoss vitamin D. Paediatric: not a prescription, a one-page rule the pharmacy can act on this month (B5, J12). Harm: the substitutes carry their own costs, and terbinafine is weaker against Microsporum. Readout: avoid-list agents prescribed per year, and lymphocyte micronucleus frequency around any unavoidable exposure. Rank: zero added risk, immediate, protects both named organs.

**3. Cyproheptadine for intake.** Step: appetite, engaging no growth-factor or steroid axis (B4). Tier: human, small paediatric series (PMID 18989154, PMID 24941960). Paediatric: approved US and EU. Phenotype: no cancer-axis engagement, which is why it outranks every hormonal appetite route. Harm: sedation, rare hepatotoxicity, and the weight gained may be fat rather than the missing muscle. Readout: weight-for-age z-score velocity with MUAC or DXA lean mass at 0 and 12 weeks, not weight alone (H5). Rank: feeding is the outcome named most often and scheduled least.

**4. Metformin oral solution.** Step: AMPK and energy stress, the approved stand-in for AICAR, which selectively removed aneuploid cells in Pallister-Killian patient fibroblasts and killed trisomic MEFs (G lead 5; Tang 2011, PMID 21315436). Tier: cell, plus human safety in another instability disorder (dossier 02 section 7). Paediatric: licensed liquid from age 10, so off-label by age here; oncology phase I range 666 to 2000 mg/m2/day, about 400 mg/day at 0.62 m2 (file I). Harm: lactic acidosis with falling filtration, and appetite suppression competing with candidate 3. Readout: interphase FISH aneuploid fraction, the more sensitive endpoint in dossier F section 3 (22 percent detectable at 3 measurements per period). Rank: the cheapest stoppable bridge from mechanism to an approved drug, and the arm the literature names as untested.

**5. Niacin.** Step: NAD+ repletion into the SIRT2-BubR1 K668 axis (North 2014, PMID 24825348; G lead 2). Tier: animal for the BubR1 link, human for the NAD+ and muscle effect (PMID 32386566), paediatric only for a different precursor in ataxia telangiectasia (G lead 4). Paediatric: approved, but no dose for this purpose. Harm: flushing, anaemia tendency, renal caution. Readout: whole-blood NAD+ with BubR1 protein in stimulated lymphocytes. Rank: the charity's own funded direction, so a family will meet it anyway, and it belongs in a protocol rather than a shop.

**6. Hydroxychloroquine.** Step: the autophagic and lysosomal dependency of aneuploid cells (Tang 2011; PMID 26404941). Tier: cell only. Paediatric: daily rheumatology use at 5 mg/kg, 90-day stable suspension (file I). Harm: retinopathy with no paediatric screening guideline, a QT stack with ondansetron every chemotherapy cycle, a renal label item, and a direction that G lead 6 shows reversed in brain. Readout: FISH aneuploid fraction. Rank: last active candidate, because the monitoring burden falls on the family.

## 2. Creative-round ideas adopted and rejected

Adopted: B2, reattribute the nephrocalcinosis to prematurity and furosemide first, because it changes prescribing today. B4, cyproheptadine over the hormonal alternatives. B5 and J12, the avoid list as a deliverable. C2, run the n-of-1 in a dish first. C3, a biomarker whose turnover matches the crossover. C4, one shared protocol across the roughly 40 patients. C10, natural-history control before drug choice. C12, a pre-specified negative-control outcome. H1 to H5, the sodium, citrate and energy plan with parent-collected measures. I, the whole pharmacist layer including its three refusals. J1, J13 and J14, family-held endpoints. G lead 5, the untested metformin arm.

Rejected: A1, digoxin for NMD stabilisation, narrow index and the truncate drops the kinase domain anyway (D section 2). A3, ataluren, not obtainable and non-renewed (file I). A9, ultra-low-dose paclitaxel, since the direction measured in this genotype class is sensitisation (PMID 19855003). B6, monthly pulsed dasatinib, no paediatric senolytic dose exists (dossier 02 section 6) and the label warns on growth. B10, dropping vinorelbine from maintenance, which discards a randomised 12.6-point overall-survival difference (PMID 31562043). C1 and J3 as written, parents dosed in vivo, which needs its own protocol and consent. J2, raising immune clearance, since the cleared compartment is the muscle and fat the child cannot spare. F section 1 as a phase decision, since a trio test replaces the table.

## 3. Drugs I exclude

Growth hormone, no efficacy in the only MVA subtype tested and predisposition treated as a contraindication (dossier 02 section 2). Mecasermin, labelled contraindication with a history of malignancy. Dasatinib with quercetin, labelled growth warning, no paediatric senolytic dose, quercetin renal reports above 1 g/day. Sirolimus and everolimus, boxed malignancy and infection warnings, and rapamycin may relieve the proteotoxic stress that limits aneuploid cells (dossier 02 section 5). Gentamicin and amikacin, nephrotoxic in the organ under treatment, whatever the clean MT-RNR1 result in file D. Hydroxyurea, raises micronucleated reticulocytes. CDK4/6 inhibitors, growth-plate and marrow effects with no paediatric indication. Colchicine, griseofulvin, acetazolamide, topiramate, loop diuretics and stoss vitamin D, which is candidate 2. High-dose nicotinamide, sirtuin inhibition opposing candidate 5, and falling platelets (A8, file I).

## 4. The strongest objection to my own top candidate

Thiazide plus citrate is not a repurposing hypothesis for MVA at all. Dossier 02 section 1 found no published report of nephrocalcinosis as an MVA feature, and B2 argues it is a prematurity and furosemide legacy. My first-ranked item is therefore ordinary paediatric nephrology, tests nothing about BubR1, and earns nothing on the hackathon's innovation criterion. I keep it first because a family will ask what changes this month, and it is the only honest answer to that question.

## 5. What would change my ranking

An RNA sample showing that the c.2210T>G transcript escapes NMD would move the allele-directed arm from prediction to measurement (D section 7) and put a readthrough candidate above metformin. Measured within-patient variance for FISH aneuploid fraction and weight velocity over 12 untreated months (C10) would tell me whether candidates 4 to 6 are testable at all. An ex vivo result in this child's or a carrier's cells showing that metformin reproduces the AICAR selection in Pallister-Killian fibroblasts would move it to second. Any paediatric senolytic dose, from any disease, reopens dasatinib. A urine work-up showing distal renal tubular acidosis or ifosfamide tubulopathy would change candidate 1's drug, not its rank. And a published MVA case of any repurposed agent given for the syndrome, of which G section 7 records none, would outweigh most of the mechanism above it.

## What a family needs to hear before a monitored n-of-1

That this is a hypothesis for follow-up and not evidence that a medicine works, in those words, because that is the hackathon's own framing (dossier 03, H1). That the prescription is off-label, which up to 90 percent of rare-disease drug use is, and that being told is their right and was withheld from one in four patients in the EURORDIS survey (E2, E3). That the information comes far enough ahead to weigh it, not at the appointment where consent is signed (N1). That the primary endpoint is one number, chosen before the first dose, with a stopping rule filed publicly (C8). That the design includes a withdrawal period, so they will be asked to stop something that may appear to be working, and that agreeing to this now is part of consent. That one outcome has been declared unchangeable by the drug, as a check on all of us (C12). That off-label use without evidence carried a 50 percent higher rate of adverse reactions in children (G1), so monitoring is the deal, not an add-on. That every result, including nothing, will be documented and shared, which is an ethical obligation and not a favour (N1). That the treating team, not this panel, prescribes. And who pays, and what happens to access if it appears to help.

## What the scientists are likely to miss

Hospital days and cannulas. A design with 3 measurements per period across 11 months (dossier F section 3) is a year of extra visits unless the draws are keyed to the surveillance visits that already exist (H5). Whether the drug is swallowable: file I is the only document in this round that asks, and it eliminates several candidates on formulation alone. That surveillance burden is accepted because it returns a sense of control (PMIDs 42303221, 20658357), so a drug that adds appointments without adding a number the family can hold subtracts from the thing they value. That the worry is cyclical around appointments (PMID 33788392), which is an argument for home-collected endpoints, not against them. That a negative result is a result families want published, and that the registry and the September 2026 audit (file G lead 10) are the place for it. That enrolling both carrier parents as research subjects (C1, J3) needs separate consent, separate results-return, and a plan for what a positive PCS finding in a parent means for their own care. And that "cancer risk reduction" is the outcome families rank second while it is the one no design here can measure, so promising it in a lay summary is the single most likely harm this report can do.

## Which creative files a family would find usable this year, and which would frighten them

Usable now: H, the menu, the sodium and citrate arithmetic and the three-day diary, because it is the only file written in a language a kitchen can use. I, the pharmacist layer, because it answers whether a thing can be given and what the pharmacy will refuse. J13, J14 and J1, home urine strips, a GBP 30 dynamometer, buccal swabs posted for blinded scoring, all of which give a number between scans. B2 and B5, the reattribution and the avoid list. C10 and C4, natural history first and one shared protocol, which is the answer to "will this help other children". G, because it names the conference, the audit and the registry, so a family can see where their data would go.

Frightening, and why: A, because it reads as a list of drugs to try on a child, including digoxin, an unlicensed import and a spindle poison, with the failure mode in small print. B6, monthly dasatinib in a growing child, because the file itself concedes three days a month may still suppress the growth plate. B10, redesigning rhabdomyosarcoma maintenance by removing an agent from a regimen with a published survival difference, which reads as trading the cancer result for a mechanistic argument. C1 and J3 in their bare form, because a parent reads "the child is not the first human dosed" as "we will dose you". F section 4, because the cells reading "above 100 percent" say in arithmetic that nothing can help, and that is not what the arithmetic means. D, because AlphaMissense scores and pLDDT values will be heard as a verdict on the child rather than a prior on a variant. J's own wrong-headed section is the exception: naming the three bad ideas out loud is the passage a family would trust most.
