# Q16. Does the aneuploidy nucleotide dependency survive as a candidate?

**Asked because** section 7 removed metformin partly on the ground that the only aneuploidy-selective killing data were Tang 2011's mouse embryonic fibroblasts, with nothing in human cells. A 2025 genome-wide screen supplies exactly that, in a pathway with marketed drugs against it. If it survives, it is the first new candidate this project has found in months.

**Source: "Aneuploidy generates enhanced nucleotide dependency and sensitivity to metabolic perturbation", Genes Dev, PMID 40324880, PMC12128873, open access, full text read.**

## Answer

**It does not survive as a candidate, and it should not. But it delivers something more useful than a candidate: the first human-cell evidence on a safety question the report had listed as open in both directions.**

### What the paper actually shows

The model is the important part. The cells are **hTERT-immortalised human mammary epithelial cells, described as "untransformed and do not contain driver mutations"**, engineered to carry breast-cancer-associated copy number changes against isogenic diploid controls. That is the closest published model to constitutional aneuploidy in otherwise normal tissue that this project has found.

- **The dependency is real and pharmacologically reachable.** A genome-wide CRISPR knockout screen found reliance on pyrimidine biosynthesis and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation. Knocking out *DHODH* reproduced it, and so did **brequinar**, a DHODH inhibitor, at 0.1 to 40 nM.
- **The selectivity is genuine.** "Diploid mammary epithelial cells are capable of fully sustaining growth using either nucleotide synthesis or nucleotide salvage alone, [but] net gain aneuploid cells cannot proliferate at full capacity using only pyrimidine salvage." Aneuploid cells "could not be fully rescued by the same dose range of uridine supplementation" that fully rescued diploid cells at physiological levels of 3 to 8 micromolar.

### Why it is not a candidate for this child

**The direction is wrong, and it is wrong in a way that is not fixable by dosing.** In a tumour, aneuploid cells are the enemy and this is a vulnerability to exploit. In mosaic variegated aneuploidy, **the aneuploid cells are the child's own normal tissue**. A drug that selectively starves aneuploid cells of pyrimidines is, in this child, a drug that selectively damages the tissue you are trying to protect. The paper's own framing is patient stratification for cancer therapy, not treatment of constitutional aneuploidy.

**Three further objections, each independently sufficient.**

1. **Brequinar is not marketed.** The marketed DHODH inhibitors are leflunomide and teriflunomide. Both are immunosuppressants, which is what they are licensed for. This report already argues that immune clearance may be one of the things restraining the aneuploid compartment, and it removed dasatinib partly on that basis. Prescribing a DHODH inhibitor would suppress that same arm while stressing the cells it is meant to remove.
2. **No evidence connects reducing aneuploid burden to reducing cancer risk.** That link is assumed by the whole aneuploidy-selective arm and has never been demonstrated in any system.
3. **The harm profile is wrong for a child with a cancer predisposition**: hepatotoxicity, teratogenicity, and elimination half-lives long enough to require an active washout procedure.

**Verdict: not a candidate, at any rank.** It is recorded in section 7 among the entries considered and rejected with a stated reason, which is where the report puts things that looked promising and are not.

### What it does change, which matters more

The same paper reports that **"aneuploid cells were significantly more sensitive to chemotherapeutic challenge than diploid cells"**, using oxaliplatin at 0.25 to 0.5 micromolar, in those same untransformed human cells.

Section 8 of the report states that whether this child tolerates standard-dose cytotoxic therapy differently from an unaffected child "is not known and is not answered by this report". There is now a first piece of human-cell evidence, in untransformed cells, that aneuploid cells are more sensitive to DNA-damaging chemotherapy.

**Three things must be said about it together, because any one alone is misleading.**

- It is about **DNA-damaging agents**, not the spindle agents that section 9 question 6 asks about. It answers a different axis of the same worry.
- It **does not distinguish tumour from normal tissue**, because both are aneuploid in this child. Greater sensitivity of normal tissue argues for anticipating toxicity; greater sensitivity of tumour tissue argues that standard doses work well. The finding does not tell you which way to move a dose, and **nothing here licenses reducing one.**
- It is consistent with, and offers a mechanism for, the only two published clinical cases in this syndrome, in both of which treating teams chose reduced-intensity regimens: reduced-intensity chemotherapy for rhabdomyosarcoma (Nishitani-Isa 2019) and reduced-intensity conditioning for transplant (Laberko 2019). Two cases and one cell-line result is a pattern worth naming and not a basis for practice.

The carve-out is unaffected and is deliberately not softened. What changes is that the report can no longer say the question is untouched by evidence: there is a signal, it points at altered chemosensitivity in both directions at once, and it belongs in front of a treating team rather than in a footnote.

## Correction applied

Section 7 gains the entry and its rejection with reasons. Section 8's statement that differential tolerance of cytotoxic therapy is unknown is qualified with this first human-cell evidence, its limits, and an explicit statement that it does not license a dose change.
