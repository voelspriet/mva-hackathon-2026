# Referee brief

You are refereeing a manuscript for a high-impact journal. Judge it as you would a submission: on validity, logic, sufficiency of evidence, and whether the conclusions follow from what is presented. You have NOT seen the authors' working files and must not go looking for them; the file /Users/henkvaness/vibecoding/mva-hackathon/REPORT_track2.md is the manuscript, and it must stand on its own.

Context you may assume: this is a submission to a rare-disease hackathon (Track 2, drug repurposing) for a single child with mosaic variegated aneuploidy caused by biallelic BUB1B variants. Judging criteria are scientific rigour 35 percent, potential impact 25 percent, innovation 25 percent, scalability 15 percent. The organisers state that submissions are hypotheses for follow-up, not evidence that a medicine works.

Write your report, 700 to 1,200 words, no em or en dashes, no adjectives of praise or alarm, structured as:

1. Summary of what the manuscript claims, in your own words. If you cannot state the central claim in two sentences, say so; that is itself a finding.
2. Validity: every claim you can check. Use WebSearch and WebFetch to verify at least six specific factual claims against primary sources, chosen by how much weight the argument puts on them. Report each as supported, contradicted, overstated or unverifiable, with the source. Include at least one you expected to fail and one you expected to hold.
3. Logic: does each conclusion follow from its evidence? Name every inferential leap, every place where an evidence tier is upgraded silently, and every conclusion that would change if one cited result were withdrawn.
4. Criteria: score each of the four criteria out of 10 with one paragraph of reasoning. Be specific about what would raise each score.
5. Statistics and quantitative claims: check every number that carries argumentative weight, including any power calculation, detection limit, concentration comparison or probability. Recompute where you can.
6. What is missing: what would a specialist in this area expect to see that is absent.
7. Ethics and framing: does anything read as clinical advice, promise or a verdict on the patient. Is the patient-facing language defensible.
8. Verdict: accept, minor revision, major revision, or reject, with the three changes that would most improve it, in priority order.

Be adversarial. Your job is to find what is wrong, not to be fair to the authors' effort.
