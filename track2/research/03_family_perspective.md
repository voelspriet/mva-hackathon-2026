# 03. Patient and family perspective on MVA and on drug repurposing

Scope: public, published text only. No contact was made with any person or organisation. Where a source names a child or family, this dossier refers to them as "a family" and records no names, ages, locations or photographs. All web sources accessed 5 September 2026 unless stated. Short quotes only.

Note on the hackathon's "BEACON": the Official Rules of the hackathon spell out the partner as "BEACON (The Benchmarking, Evaluation, and Assessment Consortium for Science)" [H3]. That is not obviously the same body as Beacon for Rare Diseases (rarebeacon.org, formerly Findacure). Section 4 covers Beacon for Rare Diseases as asked, but a report should not assert that the UK charity sponsors this hackathon without confirming it.

## 1. The MVA Society

**Mission.** The home page carries the strapline "Funding Research. Building a Community. Finding a Cure" and describes the charity as "the only organisation in the world dedicated to Mosaic Variegated Aneuploidy (MVA) an ultra-rare genetic condition affecting fewer than 50 people worldwide" [M1]. The About page says it was founded after one family's diagnosis revealed "a complete lack of treatments, medical guidance or expert support", and that it aims to sponsor research targeting actual treatments rather than academic research, connect families to experts, and build a global community [M2]. The NORD organisation listing quotes a goal of "a treatment by 2030" [M7].

**Number of known patients.** The site uses two figures side by side: "Around 40 known cases globally" and "fewer than 50 people worldwide" [M1, M4]. NORD's listing gives the breakdown "around 25 cases in the UK/EU and around the same number in the rest-of-the-world" [M7]. The 2025 patient leaflet says only that prevalence "is unknown" [M6].

**Research funded or called for.** The Why Research Matters page lists the strategies: "Identifying targets for therapies that can stabilise cells" and lower cancer risk, "Repurposing existing drugs to fast-track potential treatments", and laying groundwork for future clinical trials. It states the current gap plainly: "there is no standard treatment, no clinical guidelines, and no long-term roadmap" [M3]. The FAQ names the funded strands as "drug repurposing", "cellular analysis" and "single-cell RNA sequencing" [M4]. The home page lists active collaborations with Creighton University Medical School (Omaha), Vall d'Hebron Institute of Oncology (Barcelona) and the MRC London Institute of Medical Sciences, and reports GBP 250k raised in 2025 with a 2026 target of GBP 325k [M1]. A search snippet of the site describes the strands as drug repurposing in London, NAD+ work at Creighton, RNA sequencing in Barcelona, and a funded PhD; the Research page itself gives only the headline "treatable pathways" (drug repurposing, cellular analysis, genetic targeting) and a medical advisory board [M5].

**Treatment outcomes named as priorities.** Better symptom management in daily life, reduced cancer risk, and eventually treatments and a cure [M3]. On current care the FAQ says a cure exists "Not yet"; care today is "early surveillance, particularly for cancer, and coordinated care with experienced specialists", and "ongoing monitoring and early cancer detection" are called vital [M4]. The leaflet recommends "on-going (3 monthly) surveillance for the pre-disposition to cancer, renal support and/or any other specific support" and lists current treatments per complication: cancer (surgery and chemotherapy), short stature (nutritional support and growth hormones), heart problems (medication, pacemaker or surgery), seizures (drugs or surgery), hydrocephalus (draining excess fluid); the "treatment pipeline" column is "NA" for most rows [M6]. The MVA Explained page notes that "most doctors will never see a case in their entire career" [M8].

**The hackathon.** No page on mvasociety.org found in this search mentions "Rare Disease, Real Kid"; the news index lists radio, marathon, podcast, dinner and conference items only [M9]. The hackathon site itself credits the MVA Society as an organising partner, uses a child's drawing "courtesy of the MVA Society", and states: "There is currently no established treatment, but the MVA Society is funding focused research to bring treatments closer. Care today means managing symptoms and fighting cancer cells" [H1]. Track 2 is defined as "existing, approved medications that are plausible candidates for further investigation", with the caveat "Submissions are hypotheses for follow-up, not evidence that a medicine works", judged on scientific rigor (35%), potential impact (25%), innovation (25%) and scalability (15%) by "researchers, clinicians, and patient advocates" [H1, H3]. The rules forbid any attempt "to recontact the data subject, data subject family members, or any points of contact at the MVA Society" and forbid publishing anything that could re-identify the family [H3]. The family's stated hope, in the organisers' words: "hoping someone can find an answer" [H2].

## 2. Public accounts by MVA families

The publicly available first-person material is thin: two family stories on the MVA Society site, the leaflet, and press or podcast items whose transcripts are not published. Themes only:

- **Diagnostic odyssey.** One story describes years in which many paediatricians "were unable to diagnose" the condition, across several institutions [S2]. The founding story says there were "no treatments, no specialists, and no reliable information to guide them" and calls that silence "not just dangerous, it was unacceptable" [S1].
- **Cancer and its treatment.** One family describes a rare childhood cancer treated with a "pioneering two-stage surgical procedure" after being told "nothing could be done"; the outcome is described as good [S1]. The leaflet confirms surgery and chemotherapy as the current cancer treatment [M6]. No public MVA account found describes the chemotherapy experience itself in detail.
- **Multi-system daily burden.** One story lists "heart problems, weakened immunity and breathing difficulties", open heart surgery, "delayed physical and mental development", being "at risk of developing cancer", and "on-going health issues that need constant care and attention" [S2]. The society's own pages add growth delay, low muscle tone, learning disabilities, and lifelong, highly variable effects [M8]. Kidney involvement appears as "renal support" in the leaflet [M6]; feeding appears as "Nutritional support" [M6].
- **Surveillance.** Three-monthly cancer surveillance is the standing recommendation [M6]; the FAQ frames it as the main protective tool available today [M4].
- **What families want from research.** Access to pioneering treatment and coordinated expert care rather than "terminal acceptance" [S1]; diagnostic clarity and continued investigation of multi-system disease [S2]; and, in the hackathon framing, "what currently-approved drugs could help" [H4]. The society's stated outcome hierarchy is symptom management first, cancer risk reduction second, cure third [M3].
- **Community as a need in itself.** "no one faces this condition alone" [M4]; the leaflet describes building "a database of patients and experiences" to drive research on detection and treatment [M6].

## 3. Published research on family priorities in paediatric cancer-predisposition syndromes

- **Surveillance is burdensome but wanted.** Parents of presymptomatic children with seven CPS (LFS, FAP, BWS and others) named "logistical, emotional, physical, and financial burdens of surveillance; however, most perceived that these burdens were outweighed by the medical and emotional advantages" (Hamilton et al., J Pediatr 2026, PMID 42303221). Parents and adolescents in Toronto felt "the benefits of surveillance outweighed perceived challenges"; worry was "cyclical, associated with appointments" and waned over time (van Engelen et al., Pediatr Blood Cancer 2021, PMID 33788392). In LFS adults, 90% believed in early detection and 84% reported "a sense of control" despite limited clinical benefit (Lammens et al., Fam Cancer 2010, PMID 20658357).
- **Who carries the load.** Maternal anxiety before and between scans, fathers' more positive expectations, adolescents' relative indifference (Elias et al., Clin Cancer Res 2024, PMID 38922635). LFS couples described "ambiguous danger", with the image "We play Russian roulette once a year, but the game is never over" (Young et al., J Psychosoc Oncol 2018, PMID 30591002).
- **Practical demands of imaging.** A-T families rated cancer screening "invaluable" but said "the responsibility for monitoring" falls too strongly on parents, and asked for preparation and child-friendly MRI (Neves et al., Health Expect 2023, PMID 36929011).
- **Uncertainty as the core burden in ultra-rare cancer-prone syndromes.** Telomere biology disorder families described a "rotating menu" of scientific, practical and personal uncertainty, with patient knowledge sometimes exceeding clinicians' (Pearce et al., SSM Qual Res Health 2024, PMID 39554689). BWS parents scored moderate to severe caregiver burden on the Zarit scale (D'Onofrio et al., Children 2024, PMID 38539377).
- **Willingness to accept risk and try treatments.** Bloom syndrome families asked for modified cancer regimens because of DNA-damage sensitivity, for targeted drugs analogous to PARP inhibitors, and for a working registry and biorepository; they were "cautiously willing" partners but discouraged by "the lack of progress and information shared" (Campbell et al., Cold Spring Harb Mol Case Stud 2018, PMC5880269). FA families' decisions on stem cell transplant tracked perceived necessity and concern about harm, with "a perceived lack of choice" and heavy weight on quality of life (Hutson et al., Health Expect 2015, PMID 23621292). A stated-choice study found parents of children "about as risk tolerant for their children as adult patients are for themselves" (Johnson et al., Risk Anal 2009, PMID 18826414). A Fanconi Cancer Foundation interview study reported that extending life "matters less than improving quality of life" and that families monitor themselves daily: "It's a constant thought" [F1].
- **Outcome measures families name.** Fewer or gentler scans, psychosocial support in the first year after diagnosis, quality of life and relationships over survival alone, and reduced toxicity of cancer treatment (sources above). No published study found asked ultra-rare CPS families specifically about repurposed generic drugs; that is a gap, not a negative finding.

## 4. Patient-organisation positions on drug repurposing

- **Beacon for Rare Diseases (formerly Findacure).** Repurposing "reduces the time needed to test the effect of drugs in people" and "reduces the cost involved", so "academic researchers can lead" trials and "patient groups and charities" can fund them; Beacon cites the Alkaptonuria Society developing "a new metric" and points to REMEDi4ALL and a Social Impact Bond [B1]. The bond model spells out the economic logic: untreated rare disease costs the NHS "surgery, care, appointments, hospital stays and monitoring", and "If the drug used for treatment is a low-cost generic drug, then the savings could be significant" [B2]. Findacure's proof of concept required scientific validity, demonstrated patient need, and modelled NHS savings sufficient to fund a trial [B3]. No published Beacon statement on N-of-1 trials was found.
- **EURORDIS.** Calls for "patient-centric drug repurposing" with patients involved from "early discovery and preclinical phase, across clinical trial design and implementation to regulatory decision making"; notes about 20% of EU orphan approvals 2000 to 2021 were repurposed drugs, and warns that researchers often "lack understanding of all the substantial patient engagement needed" [E1]. Its off-label Q&A says a prescriber should "Analyse the level of evidence", "Consider the risk-benefit ratio", consider the impact of use or non-use, and "Inform the patient that the prescription is off-label"; it notes "up to 90% of drug use for rare conditions is off-label" [E2]. A 2012 EURORDIS survey found 1 in 4 patients were not told a drug was off-label and 3 in 4 wanted more benefit-risk information [E3].
- **Cures Within Reach.** Preference for rare disease, "off-patent" drugs, and "proof of concept repurposing clinical trials supported by strong scientific evidence or clinical observations" completable "in 3 years or less and for less than $250,000"; it has run a pediatric rare disease CureAccelerator [C1, C2].
- **Every Cure.** Programmes must show "high likelihood of success, potential for patient impact, and a feasible path to reaching patients in a timely and resource efficient manner", reviewed by an independent Scientific Advisory Council, with disease organisations partnered "from the very beginning" [EC1].
- **Academic synthesis (Jonker et al., Front Med 2024, PMC10828010).** Success factors include known "safety profiles, and interactions", suitable formulation (the propranolol case needed "pediatric formulations" and used the PUMA route), access to off-label and past-trial data, and patient groups as "drivers" who "bring together the parties and funds" [J1].

Common criteria across these bodies: a documented safety record, generic or low-cost status, a plausible mechanism plus clinical observation, a measurable endpoint families care about, a feasible route to access (formulation, prescriber, reimbursement), and patient involvement from the start.

## 5. Ethical guidance on N-of-1 use of off-label drugs in children with ultra-rare disease

- **Research or care?** Defelippe et al. (Drug Discov Today 2023, PMID 37356616) place n-of-1 strategies "at the intersection between medical research and clinical care" and set standards for "responsible optimized clinical n-of-1 strategies with (off-label) treatments for rare diseases". The aggregated n-of-1 model (Vrinten et al., BMJ Open 2015, PMID 26185179) shows ethics approval, registration, a validated primary outcome, blinding and placebo where feasible, and recording of "patients' and caregivers' experiences".
- **Document and share every case.** The individualized-therapy roadmap (Jonker et al., Ther Adv Rare Dis 2025, PMID 40438698) states "there is an ethical imperative to document, share, and analyze data from that case", asks that families be informed "some considerable time in advance to be able to evaluate and weigh the burdens, risks, uncertainties, and potential benefits", and calls for long-term follow-up and equitable access [N1].
- **FDA.** The 2022 draft guidance on ethical considerations for paediatric investigations requires a component analysis of risk, consent and assent, and a prospect of direct benefit when risk exceeds minimal; its pediatric ethicist notes that for rare diseases seen mainly in children "nonclinical data alone might support prospect of direct benefit" [FDA1].
- **UK prescribing.** GMC and MHRA guidance: prescribe off-label only when "a licensed alternative would not meet the patient's needs and that there is sufficient evidence or experience to demonstrate safety and efficacy"; document the justification and "agreed monitoring arrangements"; review "continued need, therapeutic response and tolerability"; report reactions via Yellow Card. Off-label use without evidence carried "a 50% higher risk of adverse drug reactions" [G1]. EMA has published evidence that off-label use in children raises the rate and seriousness of adverse reactions [EMA1].
- **Nuffield Council on Bioethics (2018).** Experimental treatment raises "Difficulties in assessing efficacy and safety; ensuring fairness of access; challenges around decision making and consent", especially when the patient is a child [NCB1].

Implication for a Track 2 report: present candidates as hypotheses (the hackathon's own wording), rank by paediatric safety record and availability, name a measurable endpoint the family already tracks (growth, scan findings, infections, hospital days), propose a monitored clinical n-of-1 design with registration and data sharing, and leave the prescribing decision to the treating team.
